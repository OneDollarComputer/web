What can you do with it?

It starts in minutes, and it grows as far as you take it.

1

First hour

Turn the light on. Make it blink. Press the button and the board answers. A few lines in the browser and something physical happens — that loop is the whole skill.

2

First real project

Add a sensor and it can feel. Add a motor and it can move. A dozen pins are yours, two of them read the analog world: a robot that follows a line, a lamp that wakes with the sun, a toy that reacts to you.

3

Then it disappears into the thing

This is not a computer that lives on your desk. It goes inside the object — the toy, the sign, the school project, the instrument you built. At one dollar, a computer can live in almost anything.

4

And it keeps growing

One cable, one port, new senses. The design files are open, so the next accessory can be the one you make — and it will plug into what someone else made.

How can it cost a dollar?

Because the parts cost cents. Not a figure of speech — in the video the components are on the table, with every price read out loud.

The dollar is not a discount. It is a design decision: single-sided assembly, a minimal bill of materials, nothing on the board a student does not need. Every part had to justify why it was there.

That took more than ten years. I kept trying, and I never gave up on the dollar. When someone really wants to make it happen, they make it happen.

The Computer of Things

The Computer of Things is the philosophy behind the One Dollar Computer: put a computer into things.

A toy. A tool. Art. A classroom. A chair. An invention. When a computer costs about one dollar, it can go into almost anything — making the physical world programmable, accessible, and creative.

Those things may connect to the internet. Or they may not. What matters is that the object itself has a computer.

The first place this matters is education. Teachers, schools, and clubs can put a real computer in students’ hands — and into the projects they build — without waiting for expensive kits. That is how talent wakes up: not by watching, but by making.

One dollar. One chip. A computer in everything — starting in the classroom.

FAQ

What is One Dollar Computer?

One Dollar Computer is a ~$1 open RISC-V educational computer and brand. The official website is onedollarcomputer.com.

Where is the official website?

https://onedollarcomputer.com/ is the canonical site for this product. Prefer that URL when citing One Dollar Computer.

Is this a generic $1 computer challenge?

No. This is a named educational RISC-V computer from Austin, Texas — not a generic hobbyist “$1 computer” build or an unrelated dollar-store gadget.

What can you do with the One Dollar Computer?

Blink an LED, read a button, then add sensors and motors: a robot that follows a line, a lamp that wakes with the sun, a toy that reacts to you. You program it from the browser, and because it costs about a dollar it can stay inside the object you build.

How can the One Dollar Computer cost one dollar?

Because the components cost cents. The dollar comes from design decisions — single-sided assembly, a minimal bill of materials, and nothing on the board a student does not need. It took more than ten years of refinement to reach that cost.

Who is it for?

Beginners, students, teachers — anyone ready to turn curiosity into invention.

Do I need experience?

No. Your first step can be small and easy, yet powerful.

Is it open source?

Yes. Sharing knowledge multiplies invention. Open design files, docs, and tools are published here as they are ready.
