Your first blink

Five steps. About two minutes. At the end, a light on your One Dollar Computer blinks — because you told it to. That is programming.

This is what you are about to make

The light blinks on, waits, off, waits. The wait is a number in your program — delay(200) means 200 milliseconds. Drag the slider and watch: the number is the speed.

200 ms
1

Open the Editor

Your first program is already written and waiting for you — the blink. You do not have to type anything yet.

Open the Editor
2

Plug in your computer

Connect your One Dollar Computer to this computer with a USB cable. No drivers, no installation — the browser does the talking.

3

Press Upload

Upload does everything: it compiles your program and sends it to the board. The first time, the browser may ask which device to use — pick your One Dollar Computer.

This is the program it sends:

use odc::*; fn main() { pin_output(LED); loop { pin_set(LED); // light on delay(200); // wait pin_clear(LED); // light off delay(200); // wait } }
4

Press the button on the board

When the editor asks, press the physical button on your One Dollar Computer. That press tells it: “receive my program.” It may take a couple of presses — keep going until the editor finds it.

5

It blinks. That was you.

The LED turns on and off, 200 milliseconds each way — exactly what the program says. Now make it yours: change delay(200) to delay(100) and press Upload again. Faster. Change it to delay(1000). Slower. You are not watching a demo — you are giving orders to a computer, and it obeys.

No board yet? Watch it on screen

Open the Editor and choose Lab → Simulate. A virtual One Dollar Computer runs the same program in your browser, LED and all — everything in the five steps works the same way when your board arrives.
