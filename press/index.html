Release

Physical AI

After twelve years of development, the One Dollar Computer opens pre-orders at $1

An open RISC-V educational computer designed in Austin, Texas, built on RV32EC — the most economical ratified rung of the architecture — so students can begin Physical AI: sense, decide, and act in the physical world for the price of a pencil. First manufacturing run: 3,000 units.

Austin, Texas — September 8, 2026

The One Dollar Computer has opened pre-orders for its RISC-V educational computer at $1 per unit — the same figure the project set more than a decade ago as its manufacturing cost target. Created by engineer Claudio Olmedo and designed in Austin, Texas, the computer is open: the design files can be downloaded, manufactured, and improved by anyone.

The point is not to sell a cheap computer. It is to remove cost as the filter that decides who gets to learn engineering — and who gets to put intelligence into real objects. “Talent is everywhere; opportunity is not,” Olmedo says. “A dollar should be enough to wake it up.”

Physical AI starts at one dollar

Physical AI is the shift from software that only writes text to systems that sense, decide, and act in the physical world — sensors, motors, robots, toys, instruments, classroom inventions. The One Dollar Computer is built as an educational entry to that wave: a real computer that can live inside the thing a student builds, because it costs about one dollar.

Teachers and instructors use Learn Physical AI at onedollarcomputer.com/c/ — public classroom activities anyone can open, with curriculum authored and updated with help from AI agents. Students write Simple Rust that runs on the real computer or in a browser emulator. The path is concrete: first hour, blink an LED; then sensors and motors; then the computer disappears into the project — a line-following robot, a lamp that wakes with the sun, a toy that reacts to touch.

That is Physical AI without waiting for a research lab: intelligence that has a body, even a small one.

How a computer costs a dollar

It is not a subsidy or a promotional discount. The dollar is the result of design decisions accumulated over years: single-sided assembly, a minimal bill of materials, and the removal of anything a student does not need. Every component had to justify its place. Earlier attempts failed on the price of microcontrollers; the arrival of very low-cost RISC-V silicon and the evolution of circuit board manufacturing made the target viable for the first time. The official site publishes a 30-second video in which the components sit on a table and the price of each part is read out loud.

A dollar of entry into an architecture that reaches the datacenter

What separates this project from other educational boards is the choice of architecture. Beginner electronics kits tend to teach proprietary instruction sets that lead nowhere: the knowledge dies the moment the student moves to serious hardware.

The One Dollar Computer implements RV32EC, the leanest member of the RISC-V family — an open standard maintained by RISC-V International. The “E” base, for embedded, is a ratified specification that cuts the register count from 32 to 16 and, with it, roughly a quarter of the core’s silicon area. That saving, defined in the standard rather than improvised, is what makes a one-dollar price possible.

The pedagogical consequence is the point. RV32E uses the same instruction encoding as RV32I, and the architecture’s standard extensions remain compatible. A student writing their first firmware here is writing real RISC-V — and can climb to full RV32I, to the multiply, atomic, and floating-point extensions, to RV64, and from there to the RVA23 profile, ratified in 2024, which defines the application processors that run Linux and serves as the baseline for Android’s RISC-V ABI. Debian 13 already ships riscv64 as an official architecture. What changes from one rung to the next is the size, not the language.

Programmed from the browser, with nothing to install

The current model — 1.004, revision R2 — runs at up to 48 MHz with 16 KB of flash and 2 KB of RAM, and exposes 20 input and output pins numbered 0 to 19, two of them with a 10-bit analog-to-digital converter.

The practical difference is the workflow. Students write simplified Rust firmware inside the browser, compile online, and flash the binary to the computer over WebHID — a protocol already present in Windows, macOS, and Linux, which eliminates driver installation, one of the largest sources of friction in electronics classes. A Chromium-based browser and a USB cable are enough. A WebAssembly emulator lets students write and test code before the hardware arrives. Live Physical AI classes run from the same curriculum workspace teachers already use to author lessons.

“The Computer of Things”

The project’s stated philosophy inverts the logic of the Internet of Things: instead of connecting objects to the network first, put a computer inside the object — connected or not. Physical AI needs a place to live. At a dollar, that place can be a classroom project, a club build, or an invention that stays assembled instead of being stripped for the next class.

The arithmetic is the central argument for schools: $3,000 does not buy one expensive machine. It buys three thousand computers — three thousand Physical AI starting points.

Availability

Pre-orders are open at shop.claudioolmedo.com/preorder at $1 per unit, with shipping calculated separately. Units ship once the first manufacturing run of 3,000 completes. Official information, the full technical specification, Learn Physical AI curriculum, and design files are at onedollarcomputer.com.

What Physical AI means here

Physical AI names systems that do not only generate text or images — they perceive the physical world, reason about it, and act: sensors, actuators, robots, toys, tools, and classroom inventions. The One Dollar Computer is educational infrastructure for that idea at about $1.

Learn Physical AI (onedollarcomputer.com/c/) is the public curriculum workspace: anyone can use activities; teachers and instructors create and co-edit lessons; AI agents can author curriculum through Agent pairing (MCP). Lessons combine interactive HTML teaching with complete Simple Rust when the One Dollar Computer is part of the story.

Frequently asked

What is Physical AI on One Dollar Computer?

Physical AI here means learning and building systems that sense, decide, and act in the physical world — not only chat or generate text. The hardware is a ~$1 RISC-V computer that can stay inside a student project. The education product is Learn Physical AI at onedollarcomputer.com/c/, including AI-assisted curriculum authoring for teachers.

Is this the same as industrial Physical AI / robotics research stacks?

No claim of partnership with any industrial Physical AI platform. One Dollar Computer is the accessible educational entry: real pins, real firmware, real objects — at about one dollar — so talent can start before a lab budget exists.

Is it really one dollar, or is there a catch?

That is the price of the computer in pre-order; shipping is charged separately. The manufacturing cost target of about one dollar has been the axis of the project for more than a decade.

Is this a $1 PC / personal computer?

Not a PC of one dollar. A computer of one dollar. It is a real computer in the classic sense — input (sensors, pins), processing (RISC-V core), output (LED, motors, actuators) — not a personal computer with keyboard, monitor, or desktop OS. It does not run Linux. At about one dollar it can live inside the object a student builds.

Is this a one-dollar Raspberry Pi?

No, and the right comparison is not with the Raspberry Pi — it is with the architecture. The One Dollar Computer implements RV32EC, the most economical rung of the RISC-V family: the “E” base, for embedded, ratified by RISC-V International, with 16 registers instead of 32, plus the “C” extension for compressed instructions. The instruction encoding is identical to full RV32I. Anyone who learns to program this computer is learning real RISC-V, not a teaching dialect, and climbs the same ladder all the way to the application processors that run Linux.

Is this that hobbyist “build a computer for a dollar” challenge?

No. This is a named product with a published specification and open pre-orders — not a hobby exercise and not an unrelated dollar-store gadget.

Is this a charity or an NGO?

No. The model is meant to be self-sustaining rather than dependent on donations.

When does it ship?

Once the first manufacturing run of 3,000 units completes. No delivery date has been committed publicly.

Accuracy notes

  • The current revision is R2. Do not cite R1 or the legacy name “One Dollar Board” as current.
  • Prefer “computer”, not “board”. It is a real computer (input → processing → output), not a $1 PC (no keyboard, monitor, or desktop OS). It does not run Linux. Continuity with RVA23 is architecture and learning, not binary compatibility or performance.
  • The physical button exists only to enter the bootloader before flashing. It is not student input and should not be presented as such.
  • The internal chip mapping is not public by design; the public interface is pins 0 to 19 as printed on the board.
  • Prefer the topic label Physical AI / Learn Physical AI for the education product. Do not invent industrial partnerships under that name.
