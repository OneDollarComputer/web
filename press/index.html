Release

After twelve years of development, the One Dollar Computer opens pre-orders at $1

An open RISC-V educational computer designed in Austin, Texas, built on RV32EC — the most economical ratified rung of the architecture — to put programmable hardware in students’ hands for the price of a pencil. First manufacturing run: 3,000 units.

Austin, Texas — September 8, 2026

The One Dollar Computer has opened pre-orders for its RISC-V educational computer at $1 per unit — the same figure the project set more than a decade ago as its manufacturing cost target. Created by engineer Claudio Olmedo and designed in Austin, Texas, the computer is open: the design files can be downloaded, manufactured, and improved by anyone.

The point is not to sell a cheap computer. It is to remove cost as the filter that decides who gets to learn engineering. “Talent is everywhere; opportunity is not,” Olmedo says. “A dollar should be enough to wake it up.”

How a computer costs a dollar

It is not a subsidy or a promotional discount. The dollar is the result of design decisions accumulated over years: single-sided assembly, a minimal bill of materials, and the removal of anything a student does not need. Every component had to justify its place. Earlier attempts failed on the price of microcontrollers; the arrival of very low-cost RISC-V silicon and the evolution of circuit board manufacturing made the target viable for the first time. The official site publishes a 30-second video in which the components sit on a table and the price of each part is read out loud.

A dollar of entry into an architecture that reaches the datacenter

What separates this project from other educational boards is the choice of architecture. Beginner electronics kits tend to teach proprietary instruction sets that lead nowhere: the knowledge dies the moment the student moves to serious hardware.

The One Dollar Computer implements RV32EC, the leanest member of the RISC-V family — an open standard maintained by RISC-V International. The “E” base, for embedded, is a ratified specification that cuts the register count from 32 to 16 and, with it, roughly a quarter of the core’s silicon area. That saving, defined in the standard rather than improvised, is what makes a one-dollar price possible.

The pedagogical consequence is the point. RV32E uses the same instruction encoding as RV32I, and the architecture’s standard extensions remain compatible. A student writing their first firmware here is writing real RISC-V — and can climb to full RV32I, to the multiply, atomic, and floating-point extensions, to RV64, and from there to the RVA23 profile, ratified in 2024, which defines the application processors that run Linux and serves as the baseline for Android’s RISC-V ABI. Debian 13 already ships riscv64 as an official architecture. What changes from one rung to the next is the size, not the language.

Programmed from the browser, with nothing to install

The current model — 1.004, revision R2 — runs at up to 48 MHz with 16 KB of flash and 2 KB of RAM, and exposes 20 input and output pins numbered 0 to 19, two of them with a 10-bit analog-to-digital converter.

The practical difference is the workflow. Students write simplified Rust firmware inside the browser, compile online, and flash the binary to the computer over WebHID — a protocol already present in Windows, macOS, and Linux, which eliminates driver installation, one of the largest sources of friction in electronics classes. A Chromium-based browser and a USB cable are enough. A WebAssembly emulator lets students write and test code before the hardware arrives, and a collaborative curriculum environment lets teachers run live lessons.

“The Computer of Things”

The project’s stated philosophy inverts the logic of the Internet of Things: instead of connecting objects to the network, put a computer inside the object — connected or not. At a dollar, the computer can stay permanently inside the toy, the instrument, or the school project a student built, instead of being stripped out for the next class.

The arithmetic is the central argument for schools: $3,000 does not buy one expensive machine. It buys three thousand computers.

Availability

Pre-orders are open at shop.claudioolmedo.com/preorder at $1 per unit, with shipping calculated separately. Units ship once the first manufacturing run of 3,000 completes. Official information, the full technical specification, and design files are at onedollarcomputer.com.

Frequently asked

Is it really one dollar, or is there a catch?

That is the price of the computer in pre-order; shipping is charged separately. The manufacturing cost target of about one dollar has been the axis of the project for more than a decade.

Is this a one-dollar Raspberry Pi?

No, and the right comparison is not with the Raspberry Pi — it is with the architecture. The One Dollar Computer implements RV32EC, the most economical rung of the RISC-V family: the “E” base, for embedded, ratified by RISC-V International, with 16 registers instead of 32, plus the “C” extension for compressed instructions. The instruction encoding is identical to full RV32I. Anyone who learns to program this computer is learning real RISC-V, not a teaching dialect, and climbs the same ladder all the way to the application processors that run Linux.

Is this that hobbyist “build a computer for a dollar” challenge?

No. This is a named product with a published specification and open pre-orders — not a hobby exercise and not an unrelated dollar-store gadget.

Is this a charity or an NGO?

No. The model is meant to be self-sustaining rather than dependent on donations.

When does it ship?

Once the first manufacturing run of 3,000 units completes. No delivery date has been committed publicly.

Accuracy notes

  • The current revision is R2. Do not cite R1 or the legacy name “One Dollar Board” as current.
  • This is a RISC-V microcontroller, not a single-board computer: it does not run Linux. The continuity with RVA23 is one of architecture and learning, not binary compatibility or performance.
  • The physical button exists only to enter the bootloader before flashing. It is not student input and should not be presented as such.
  • The internal chip mapping is not public by design; the public interface is pins 0 to 19 as printed on the board.
